This article discusses the use of balloon ascents for observational purposes. One important discovery during the time this article was published was that there exists a warm current of air at 18,000 feet that sweeps from the southwest to the northeast. The French showed "the practical value of the balloon" during the siege of Paris. (As the author notes, "The French have hitherto undoubtedly held the foremost rank in aerial navigation.") French newspapers of that time discussed the idea of using aeronauts as surveyors. Though aerial photography had been around for many years, executing a plan of this magnitude would cost an estimated $3.5 million for the entire United States, the author goes on to say.

The latter half of the article discusses an ascent made by Th&#233;odore Sivel and Joseph Croc&#233;-Spinelli on March 22, 1874, "under the auspices of the Society for Aerial Navigation." They carried with them "a considerable quantity of oxygen, inclosed in suitable vessels, and inhaled by means of a tube." Of the many observations made by Sivel and Croc&#233;-Spinelli during this ascent, this article highlights two. One was 12,500 feet, where the two aeronauts passed a "suspended cloud of ice crystals, which glittered in the sun, but were so perfectly translucent that a clear view of the panorama below the balloon was seen." The second observation was that the lines indicating water in the solar spectrum were confirmed to be from moist vapors in the atmosphere; the solar spectrum showed no water at all in the dry air of the upper atmosphere.
