This copy, bound in half leather with green cloth boards, contains three annual reports of the Aeronautical Society of Great Britain. The Society's first meeting in 1869 was held on May 20th in the Theatre of the Society of Arts in London, and the notes were compiled in the *Fourth Annual Report of the Aeronuatical Society of Great Britain*. The introductory remarks for this year state the importance of continued experimentation in the field of aeronautics. Captain John Murray (1828-1872) suggested offering a prize of no less than &#163;100 to solve the following problem: "Required, the relation between the velocity of a current of air and its pressure on a plain surface of given size, shape, and inclination." Aside from discussions on how to conduct experiments and how to construct an aerial machine, the Fourth Annual Report includes a reprint of M. de Lucy's "On the Flight of Birds, of Bats, and of Insects, in Reference to the Subject of Aerial Locomotion," translated from the French by Cornelius B. Fox. It is noted that this paper was circulated among the members because of demand and because it was out of print at the time.

The concluding remarks for the Fourth Annual Report mention three topics that attracted attention over in 1869. First, an airship that was built sometime in the previous year in San Francisco by Frederick Marriott, who previously worked with John Stringfellow on "aeronautical pursuits." The following details were given to the Society. Sustaining planes were to be placed on either side of a balloon to "bouy up the weight of the entire machine." A steam engine would be used to propel the ship forward. It was hoped that "this experiment will practically test that which has been a long cherished idea with some, viz., to employ the balloon merely as a means of partly sustaining the weight, and the surplus to be carried by the mechanical force employed in the machine."

The second topic was the projected voyage of Alexander Chevalier across the Atlantic. His preparations for the voyage were recorded in the *Chicago Times*. However, no attempt was made, and the Society wondered if the enterprise had been abandoned. The third topic was regarding the Henri Giffard's captive balloon at Ashburnham Park. On a calm day, this balloon could ascend to a height of 2,000 feet. Nearly 30 ascents were made, and the observations from these ascents can be found in *Transactions of the Sections in the Report of the Meeting of the British Association in Exeter, 1869*.

Other important remarks include a short obituary of Charles Green, who died in his home on March 26, 1870, and mention of the creation of an Aeronautical Society in New York. No details are given in the Annual Report about the society's mission or membership. It is not clear if this Aeronautical Society is an offshoot of the Aeronautical Society of Great Britain or a new society altogether.

The first meeting of the Aeronautical Society of Great Britain in 1870 was held on the 3rd of June. It began with a statement from the chairman that not much progress had been made over the past year because of a lack of experimental data. Walter Clare echoed this statement when read his paper titled "A Glance at Aeronautical Science," saying that reading the previous year's report is "somewhat tinged with a shade of the melancholy when we consider how little has really been done in that time&mdash;not one new theory has been advanced, not one experiment of value made." During this meeting, Major J. Scott Phillips exhibited a model of his aerial machine and read a paper about a steam engine he had invented to power the machine.

A second general meeting was held on the 13th of July, with more attendance than usual. More details about Mr. Marriott's flying machine were shared courtesy of a society member living in India who had corresponded with Marriott. A full account of the model "Avitor" can be found in the July 31, 1869, issue of *San Francisco Times*. The model was 37 feet long and 12 feet wide, and the cylinder was 8 feet in diameter. The model was exhibited at the Mechanics' Pavilion and experiments carried out at Shellmound Park in Emeryville, CA. With an experimental engine with less than a quarter horsepower, the machine was able to travel in any direction, including against "a strong current of air." The fifth annual report includes an English translation (by T. J. Bennett) of Pieter Harting's paper "Observations Upon the Relative Size of the Wings and the Weight of the Pectoral Muscles in the Vertebrated Flying Animals." The concluding remarks speak of "the unprecedented practical employment of balloons for mails and passengers from beseiged fortresses, and over hostile territories" over the past year. Many communications on aerial navigation were sent to the Society, and there was demand for more knowledge about the atmosphere itself. The aim of the Society was "connecting the velocity of the air with it pressure on plane surface at various inclinations." The important of experimentation was stressed since mathematical theory had yielded no fruitful results.

The meeting for 1871 was held on the 17th of July. The siege of Paris was mentioned in the introductory remarks and how it had "set men's minds at work on the subject of aerial locomotion." The Society, through Francis Herbert Wenham, directed a flying machine to be constructed by John Browning. Several papers were read by various Society members. The concluding remarks discuss the navigation of balloons and also includes a table of horizontal and veritical forces on inclinded planes at various angles. The description of this "instrument" (i.e., wind tunnel), which waa designed by Wenham, is as follows: "By means of a fan-blower, a current of considerable force was directed through a trunk ten feet long by eighteen inches square. The plane to be acted upon was fixed to the long end of a horizontal arm, which vibrated like the beam of a balance, and bore upon its shorter end a sliding counterweight, so as to balance the weight of any plane which might be fixed at the opposite extremity. The horizontal or direct pressure was read off by a spring steelyard, which was connected to the end of a lever from a vertical spindle close to the base of the machine. The vertical or raising force due to the various inclinations was read off by an upright spring steelyard." The results showed that "the lifting force of inclined planes, carried horizontally through air, is increased in the direct ratio that the sine bears to the length of the plane, or the height of the incline to the base." The concluding remarks end with a note that no successful attempts in aerial locomotion had been made over the past year, though experiments to find a "law of action and propulsion of bodies sustained in air" have greatly improved.
